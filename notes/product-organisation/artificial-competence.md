AI is widening the gap between what people can *do* and what they can *judge*. Historically we could do fewer things than we could evaluate, which kept us safe — we knew when to defer to experts. AI now lets anyone perform tasks (code, design, legal docs, prioritization) at a mediocre level, even in areas where their judgement is weak, creating a "danger zone" where people can't tell if the AI's output is any good. Cognitive biases (better-than-average effect, Dunning-Kruger) mean people rarely self-police this gap. The proposed fix — AI-as-a-judge — helps but is double-edged: it can catch blind spots, but also enables everyone to use AI to critique and "improve" everyone else's work, including work already produced by AI, raising questions about role boundaries, expertise, and accountability in the workplace.

> "How happy are you in this near-future world, where AI empowered everyone to critique and "improve" everyone else's work? At what points does this world become disempowering or even suffocating?"

---

> **Why this matters:** When everyone is talking about using AI to do the work of other roles, how one judges the quality becomes the first problem. And even if AI-as-a-judge is applied, it will at some point become disempowering when everyone uses AI to judge others' work produced by AI.
