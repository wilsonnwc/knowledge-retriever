## Part 1: Fundamental Design Principles

**Affordances & Signifiers**

Affordances represent the possibilities in the world for how an agent (a person, animal, or machine) can interact with something. Some affordances are perceivable, others are invisible. Signifiers are signals that communicate what's possible.

Good design requires good communication of the purpose, structure, and operation of a device. The role of the signifier is to make visible both what users can do and what will happen when they do it. Examples: signs labeled "push," "pull," or "exit" on doors; arrows indicating direction.

> **Why this matters:** Affordances are what's possible; signifiers communicate it. Good design makes both visible.

**Conceptual Models**

Mental models are the conceptual models in people's minds that represent their understanding of how things work. Good conceptual models are the key to understandable, enjoyable products: good communication is the key to good conceptual models.

> **Why this matters:** The user's mental model is the foundation of good UX; communication builds that model.

**Human-Centered Design**

The discipline of human-centered design ensures that the result fits human desires, needs, and capabilities. The process:
1. Spend time determining what basic, fundamental issue needs to be addressed
2. Don't search for solutions until you've determined the real problem
3. Consider a wide range of potential solutions before converging on a proposal (diverge-converge model)

This process is called design thinking. Designers should focus attention on cases where things go wrong, not just when things work as planned. When a system fails elegantly, highlights the problem, and lets the user fix it — that collaboration feels wonderful.

> **Why this matters:** Design thinking is diverge-converge; the joy is in elegant failure recovery, not flawless execution.

## Part 2: Human Psychology & Behavior

**Cognition & Emotion**

Emotion is highly underrated. The emotional system is a powerful information processing system that works in tandem with cognition:
- Cognition attempts to make sense of the world
- Emotion assigns value

The emotional system determines whether a situation is safe or threatening, whether something is good or bad, desirable or not. Cognition provides understanding; emotion provides value judgments. A human without working emotion has difficulty making choices. A human without cognition is dysfunctional.

For designers, the most critical aspect is that every action is associated with an expectation.

> **Why this matters:** Emotion isn't decoration—it's how humans assign value and make decisions. Ignore it and you build systems that confuse.

**Flow State**

The flow state occurs when the challenge of an activity just slightly exceeds the person's skill level, so full attention is continually required. Flow requires that the activity be neither too easy nor too difficult. The constant tension coupled with continual progress can be an engaging, immersive experience.

> **Why this matters:** Engagement happens at the edge of competence; design for this balance, not for ease.

**Seven Stages of Action**

A mental model for how users think through tasks:
1. What do I want to accomplish?
2. What are the alternative action sequences?
3. What action can I do now?
4. How do I do it?
5. What happened?
6. What does it mean?
7. Is this okay? Have I accomplished my goal?

This reveals where design can help bridge the gulf between intention and execution.

> **Why this matters:** Understand the user's mental journey; design reduces friction at each stage.

**Human Error (Reframe)**

What we call "human error" is often simply a human action that is inappropriate for the needs of technology. This flags a deficit in our technology, not in the person. We should eliminate the concept of error: instead, realize that people can use assistance in translating their goals and plans into the appropriate form for technology.

> **Why this matters:** Reframe: "human error" is usually a design failure. The system should adapt to humans, not blame them.

## Part 3: Practical Design Patterns

**Error Messages**

Eliminate all error messages from electronic or computer systems. Instead, provide help and guidance. Errors are design failures; replace blame with guidance.

> **Why this matters:** Errors signal design problems, not user incompetence.

**Request for Confirmation**

The request for confirmation seems like an irritant rather than an essential safety check because people tend to focus on the action rather than the object being acted upon. A better approach: display both the action to be taken and the object, perhaps with choices of "cancel" or "do it." Make salient what the implications of the action are.

> **Why this matters:** Confirmation dialogs fail when they don't show what will change; good UX makes consequences visible.

**"Good Enough" & Strategic Trade-offs**

If the product has real strengths, it can afford to just be "good enough" in the other areas. Excel in one area and you can afford to be ordinary elsewhere.

> **Why this matters:** Not every feature needs to be perfect; strategic trade-offs are legitimate design choices.

## Part 4: Systems Thinking & Resilience

**Five Whys**

The Japanese have long followed a procedure for getting at root causes called the "Five Whys," originally developed by Sakichi Toyoda for the Toyota Production System. When searching for the reason, do not stop at the first answer: ask why that was the case, then ask why again. Keep asking until you have uncovered the true underlying causes. (It doesn't always take exactly five.)

> **Why this matters:** Deep root cause analysis requires relentless questioning; the first "why" is rarely the real problem.

**Swiss Cheese Model of Accidents**

Accidents usually have multiple causes. The British accident researcher James Reason describes this through the metaphor of slices of Swiss cheese: unless the holes all line up perfectly, there will be no accident. Two lessons:
1. Do not try to find "the" cause of an accident
2. Decrease accidents by designing systems with: extra precautions against error (more slices of cheese), fewer opportunities for slips/mistakes (less holes), and different mechanisms in different subparts (ensuring holes don't align)

Design redundancy and layers of defense. That's Swiss cheese.

> **Why this matters:** Complex systems fail through alignment of independent failures, not single causes. Resilience comes from redundancy and diversity.

**Resilience Engineering**

Resilience engineering is a paradigm for safety management that focuses on how to help people cope with complexity under pressure. It contrasts with the typical approach of tabulating errors and intervening to reduce the count.

A resilient organisation treats safety as a core value, not a commodity that can be counted. Safety shows itself only by the events that do NOT happen. Rather than view past success as a reason to ramp down investments, resilient organisations continue to invest in anticipating the changing potential for failure. They know their knowledge is imperfect and the environment constantly changes.

One measure of resilience is the ability to create foresight—to anticipate the changing shape of risk before failure and harm occurs.

> **Why this matters:** True safety is invisible and requires constant investment in foresight; it can't be managed by counting incidents.

**Checklists**

Checklists are powerful tools, proven to increase the accuracy of behavior and reduce error, particularly slips and memory lapses. They are especially important in situations with multiple, complex requirements, and even more so where there are interruptions. With multiple people involved, it is essential that lines of responsibility be clearly spelled out. It is always better to have two people do checklists together as a team: one to read the instruction, the other to execute it.

> **Why this matters:** Checklists are underrated operational tools; pair-execution is critical when stakes are high.

## Part 5: On Process Reality

The day a product development process starts, it is behind schedule and above budget.

> **Why this matters:** Accept this as reality, not failure. Plan accordingly.
