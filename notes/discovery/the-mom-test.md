"3 simple rules of the Mom Test:
1. Talk about their life instead of your idea
2. Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future
3. Talk less and listen more

Bad questions:
* Do you think it's a good idea?
    * Self-indulgent, high risk of false positives
* Would you buy a product which did X?
    * Instead, ask "did you solve this problem last time, are you searching for solutions still?"
* Would you pay X for a product which did Y?
    * People are overly optimistic about what they would do.

Good questions:
* Talk me through the last time that happened.
* How are you dealing with it now?
* Are you actively searching for a (replacement) tool to solve the problem?
* Why do you bother?
    * Understand their motivations
* Is there anything else I should have asked?
* Who else should I talk to?

Fluffs:
* Generic claims ("I usually", "I always", "I never")
* Future-tense promises ("I would", "I will") 
* Hypothetical maybes ("I might", "I could")
* Worst: "I would definitely buy that"

"Does-this-problem-matter" questions:
* How seriously do you take your blog?
* Do you make money from it?
* Have you tried making more money from it?
* How much time do you spend on it each week?
* Do you have any major aspirations for your blog?
* Which tools and services do you use for it?
* What are you already doing to improve this?
* What are the 3 big things you're trying to fix or improve right now?

Customer Slicing
* If you're facing a generic or varied set of customer segments, you can use Customer Slicing to pick a concrete starting point. Start with any segment and then keep slicing off better and better sub-sets of it until you've got a tangible sense of who you can go talk to and where you can find them. 
* Start with a broad segment and ask: 
    * Within this group, which type of person would want it most? 
    * Would everyone within this group buy/use it, or only some? 
    * Why does that sub-set want it? (e.g. what is their specific problem) 
    * Does everyone in the group have that motivation or only some? 
    * What additional motivations are there? 
    * Which other types of people have these motivations?
* If there isn't a clear physical or digital location at which you can find your customer segment, then it's probably still too broad.
* You'll broaden your segment back out later. But your learning will go faster (and be more useful) for now by choosing someone who is specific and who also and meets the three big criteria of being reachable, profitable, and personally rewarding.

Tips:
* Don't fish for compliments - deflect them:
    * Go back to ask questions about how they are currently dealing with the problem, find out if they are just trying to end the conversation by playing nice
* Some people are a complainer, not a customer.  They only imagine but never actually go find a solution actively.
* Ideas and feature requests should be understood, but not obeyed.
* If you've mentioned your idea, people will try to protect your feelings.
* Switch out from pitch mode
    * "I just slipped into pitch mode. I'm really sorry about that—I get excited about these things. Can we jump back to what you were just saying? You were telling me that…"
* Every time you talk to someone, you should be asking at least one question which has the potential to destroy your currently imagined business.
* Don't obsess over unimportant nuances, don't prematurely zoom in on a non-problem.  Understand the big picture, see if the interviewee sees the problem as a must-solve.
* Start broad and don't zoom in until you've found a strong signal, both with your whole business and with every conversation.
* If you've run more than 10 conversations and are still getting results that are all over the map, then it's possible that your customer segment is too vague, which means you're mashing together feedback from multiple different types of customers.
* Before we can serve everyone, we have to serve someone. Forgetting about all the possibilities and focusing on who would most likely buy."

> **Why this matters:** The Mom Test is a foundational book on user research interviews — specifically how to avoid false positives and get genuine signal from customers without them just telling you what you want to hear.
